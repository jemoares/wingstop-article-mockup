Can AI make recruitment more human? Wingstop Australia thinks so.

As retailers turn to AI to tackle recruitment pressures, Wingstop Australia is showing how automation can speed up hiring without losing the personal nuance.

In collaboration with Workday

[Publish date]  •  8 min read

Greater recruitment efficiency presents a dilemma. For candidates, automated recruitment can feel impersonal, reducing what should be a human interaction to a series of questions, automated responses and algorithmic decisions.

The challenge for retailers is not simply how much of the hiring process they can automate, but also how to make the experience better for the people going through it.

It’s a question becoming more pressing as the industry faces growing recruitment demands. Retail trade vacancies rose 24.5 per cent in the three months to August 2026, the largest percentage increase of any industry, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics.

For Wingstop Australia, which opened its first store in 2025 and now has six, with plans for 110, the challenge was particularly acute.

Each new store requires between 40 and 80 crew members. With a new opening planned approximately every six weeks, the business needed a recruitment process that could keep pace with its expansion without overwhelming store managers or alienating potential employees.

Jono Spragg, Wingstop Australia’s Director of Operations, was initially sceptical about using AI in recruitment. Eighteen months ago, he says, he would have “switched off” at the suggestion of a bot managing the hiring conversation.

But years working in hospitality had also shown him how inefficient traditional recruitment could be.

Managers would post advertisements on Indeed, work through hundreds of résumés and often rely on personal familiarity when deciding whom to interview. Phone calls frequently reached candidates who had forgotten they had applied, and arranging a single interview could take two weeks.

At Wingstop’s planned rate of expansion, that approach would quickly become a bottleneck.

The business turned to Workday Paradox, a conversational AI recruitment assistant, implementing the technology before its first Australian store opened.

Rather than deploying a generic recruitment bot, Wingstop customised the experience around its own brand, creating “Ranchy”, a talking tub of ranch dressing that guides applicants through the hiring process.

The company deliberately made it clear that candidates were interacting with a bot, while tailoring its language to feel conversational and approachable. The system also needed to comply with Australian legal requirements and integrate with Wingstop’s payroll software.

Spragg established a guiding principle: make the initial conversation “as automated as possible”, with “human feeling language on top of it”.

The approach was not about removing people from recruitment. It was about removing the repetitive administrative tasks that prevented managers from spending time with candidates.

Wingstop’s experience offers five practical lessons in how automation can make recruitment faster, more consistent and potentially more personal.

1. Make the first interaction faster and fairer

For many candidates, the first hurdle in recruitment is simply getting their application seen.

Ranchy sits on Wingstop’s careers page and asks each applicant approximately 14 questions, covering matters such as working rights and whether they can meet the physical requirements of the role.

These are straightforward questions, but Spragg says identifying issues late in the process can cost managers a week.

By establishing the screening criteria before applications arrive, Wingstop ensures every candidate is assessed against the same initial requirements.

Spragg says the approach also removes some of the personal biases that previously influenced his own first assessment of applicants, an important consideration for a business that places a strong emphasis on diversity.

At King’s Cross, Wingstop received approximately 800 applications for 50 positions. Rather than manually reviewing every résumé, Spragg interviewed candidates who had already cleared the initial screening process.

The benefit is not simply time saved. A consistent first screening process also reduces the reliance on individual managers’ subjective judgments about whom to progress.

2. Remove the friction from scheduling interviews

One of the most frustrating parts of recruitment, for candidates and employers alike, is the back and forth involved in arranging an interview.

A process that should take minutes can stretch into days or weeks, particularly when store managers are balancing recruitment with operational responsibilities.

Wingstop uses Ranchy to offer candidates three available interview times across three days, drawn directly from Spragg’s calendar. If none is suitable, applicants can access the full calendar to find an alternative.

Spragg says the wait between application and interview is generally no more than three days, although some candidates choose later appointments.

Other employers using the same technology have reported similar improvements.

Singtel, which recruits approximately 4,000 people annually, says its fastest interview was booked in nine seconds. Its interview rescheduling rate has also fallen from 19 per cent to 10 per cent, supported by automated meeting reminders.

For candidates, the value is straightforward: fewer delays, less uncertainty and greater control over when they can attend an interview.

3. Meet candidates where they already are

Recruitment processes are often designed around the convenience of employers rather than the circumstances of applicants.

For frontline retail roles, candidates are frequently applying on their phones, between other commitments or while travelling.

Spragg has even interviewed candidates while they were sitting on a train.

Wingstop has adapted its recruitment approach to reflect that reality.

New store locations display a large image of Ranchy alongside a QR code, allowing prospective employees to begin an application directly from their phones.

The conversational assistant is available around the clock, removing the need to engage with a hiring manager during business hours.

Singtel similarly identifies round-the-clock access as a benefit for both candidates and recruitment teams, while the English Schools Foundation in Hong Kong uses the same technology to answer routine applicant questions.

Wingstop has also created different recruitment pathways for different positions. A general manager applicant, for example, receives a different set of questions from someone applying for a crew role.

The principle is to make the process accessible and relevant to the person applying, rather than forcing every candidate through the same experience.

4. Make the transition from candidate to employee seamless

Recruitment does not end when a candidate accepts an offer.

For many retailers, the administrative work involved in bringing someone into the business can create further delays, particularly when information must be entered into multiple systems.

Wingstop has connected its recruitment platform directly to Tanda, its payroll system, allowing information collected during the application process to flow into employee records without being entered twice.

The process collects tax file numbers, superannuation details, evidence of working rights and parental consent for employees under 16.

Once a candidate signs an offer, payroll and training onboarding begin automatically.

Wingstop pays its employees weekly, and Spragg says the integrated process helps ensure new starters are paid from their first pay cycle.

“We’ve never messed up a payroll.”

Jono Spragg, Director of Operations, Wingstop Australia

He has seen the consequences of late paperwork in previous hospitality roles, where administrative delays could affect an individual’s first pay and sometimes the pay of an entire team.

While much of this work happens behind the scenes, it has a direct impact on the employee experience.

A smooth transition from applicant to employee means new starters can focus on their role rather than chasing paperwork or worrying about when they will be paid.

5. Use automation to create a more personal first day

Perhaps the clearest example of Wingstop’s approach comes after the recruitment process is complete.

During onboarding, Ranchy asks each new employee for their uniform size and favourite drink.

When they arrive for their first shift, their correctly sized uniform is ready and their preferred drink is waiting.

The team then sits down with the new starter over that drink to begin their introduction to the business.

It is a small gesture, but one that Spragg believes makes a meaningful difference.

He remembers the alternative from his years working in pubs and restaurants: a new employee arriving to find an oversized leftover uniform and a supervisor who had forgotten they were starting.

A poor first-day experience has consequences, he argues, because “their care drops off and their learning curve drops off”.

Spragg says Wingstop’s employee retention is very high and credits the quality of the first-day experience as one contributing factor, although he did not provide a figure.

It is also an example of how automation can support more thoughtful human interactions.

By collecting basic information in advance and ensuring the practical details are organised, the technology gives managers more opportunity to focus on welcoming a new colleague.

Where should retailers start?

For Wingstop, the operational results have been significant.

  • At King’s Cross, the entire process from application to onboarding took five days, with 50 positions filled from approximately 800 applications.
  • At Parramatta Square, the business received 1,200 applications in just ten days.

The efficiency gains extend beyond the recruitment team.

Another store-based employer using Workday Paradox, which asks individual store managers to manage their own recruitment, reported that automating the early stages returned more than 40,000 hours a week to store leaders and reduced time to hire from more than ten days to fewer than five.

But Spragg’s advice to retailers considering a similar approach is not simply to introduce technology and expect immediate results.

He recommends starting with a clear understanding of the people the business wants to attract, then investing time in the language and experience of the recruitment process.

For Wingstop, where most crew members are casual or part-time and many want to begin working quickly, timing is particularly important.

The business typically advertises for two weeks and interviews over the following two, recognising that candidates who face lengthy delays before starting are more likely to move on.

Spragg describes the implementation as a change management exercise and encourages leaders to build confidence within their teams by demonstrating practical improvements along the way.

He also believes retailers need to recognise that younger employees are increasingly comfortable interacting with automated systems.

“They’re all using it. It’s speeding up their life, and so you’ll be left behind.”

Jono Spragg, Director of Operations, Wingstop Australia

Yet Wingstop’s experience suggests the opportunity is more nuanced than simply replacing manual recruitment with AI.

The real value lies in identifying where automation can remove delays, reduce inconsistency and improve the experience for candidates, while preserving the human interactions that shape their first impression of an employer.

For retailers, the question may no longer be whether AI belongs in recruitment, but how to ensure the process still feels human.
